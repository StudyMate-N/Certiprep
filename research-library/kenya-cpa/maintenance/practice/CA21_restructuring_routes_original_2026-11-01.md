# CA21 — Original company rescue and insolvency routes practice (2026-11-01)

**Status:** original fictional learning exercise, not a KASNEB past paper or legal advice. Selected arithmetic verified; Kenyan legal specialist and independent academic review pending.

## Case A: Distress is a warning, not a statutory finding
Kijani Components Ltd (fictional) has current assets of KES 8,400,000 and current liabilities of KES 10,100,000. Forecast 30-day receipts are KES 2,400,000 and essential payments KES 3,100,000. A potential investor discusses KES 500,000 but makes no binding commitment.

**Questions:** Compute net working capital and forecast cash gap. Should the potential funding be treated as available? What evidence is needed before choosing a legal rescue route?

**Worked answer:** Net working capital = 8,400,000 − 10,100,000 = **KES −1,700,000**. Cash gap = 2,400,000 − 3,100,000 = **KES −700,000**. The KES 500,000 proposal is not committed cash; even if it arrived, a **KES −200,000** shortfall remains. Obtain current financial statements, realistic forecasts, security/creditor schedules, funding commitments, a rescue plan and advice on applicable statutory tests. Neither a negative working-capital balance nor this short cash forecast alone establishes the legal insolvency test.

## Case B: A company seeking breathing space
A fictional company has viable orders but imminent creditor enforcement. Directors want to evaluate refinancing while continuing operations.

**Worked answer:** A **pre-insolvency moratorium** may merit professional consideration. The BRS practitioner guide describes a company remaining under directors' management while a licensed insolvency-practitioner monitor supervises. Do not promise that the company qualifies, that all enforcement stops, or that the guide's procedure/duration is the current law. Verify eligibility, court filing, notices, extension and funding tests against the latest Act and regulations.

## Case C: Proposal is not approval
A fictional company prepares a staged creditor repayment proposal; creditors have not approved it and no court adoption has occurred. Another has no viable rescue plan and considers closing.

**Worked answer:** A **company voluntary arrangement (CVA)** involves a creditor compromise/restructuring proposal; BRS describes creditor consideration and court adoption. A draft proposal is **not automatically an approved binding CVA**. Liquidation is a distinct process involving realisation and distribution of assets under legal priorities. Neither option can be chosen solely from this narrative without reviewing facts, statute and legal advice.
